# Benchmark: robotics product and platform websites

Research date: 2026-10-04. Method: pages were read with a text fetcher (home, product, about/company, docs or SDK where they exist). Where a page could not be fetched it is marked **[not fetched; search summary]**. Every capability statement below is what the site says about itself, not a verified fact. At most one short quoted phrase per source; everything else is paraphrased. No layouts, assets or code were copied.

Sites covered: Figure (figure.ai, /index-app), 1X (1x.tech), Unitree (unitree.com), Pollen Robotics (pollen-robotics.com), NVIDIA Isaac (developer.nvidia.com/isaac plus Isaac Sim, Isaac Lab, GR00T).

## 1. Figure — figure.ai and figure.ai/index-app

Pages read: home, /figure (Figure 03), /helix, /company, /master-plan, /news/introducing-figure-03, /index-app. (`/figure-03` returns 404; the product page lives at `/figure`.)

- **What problem it solves.** Figure frames the problem as labour: unsafe, dull or unfilled jobs in warehouses and factories first, then household chores. The Master Plan page claims roughly 10 million such US jobs and 2.3 billion homes as the eventual market. Index-app addresses a second problem: the shortage of real-world video data needed to train humanoids.
- **How it approaches it.** One general-purpose humanoid (Figure 03) plus one in-house AI stack (Helix, described as a vision-language-action model that runs perception, reasoning and movement on board). Index is a two-sided mobile network: people film themselves doing everyday tasks and get paid per minute; other people book those same creators for services now, with the site promising robots later. The company page shows a three-generation lineage (F.01 to F.03) and three phases: build the body, master manipulation, enter the labour force.
- **What it offers and to whom.** The site offers no purchase path. It offers a vision to investors and press, a "Careers" funnel to engineers, a newsletter and contact form to prospective enterprise customers, and an app to anyone with a phone who wants to earn money filming chores. The site claims 264K app downloads, $15M paid out across 108 countries.
- **How it communicates.** Headlines are short, declarative and consumer-flavoured on the home page; the announcement post is long-form engineering prose with varied sentence length. Navigation is flat: Figure 03, Helix, Index, News, Careers, Company. Proof is mostly video (eight-item carousel of demos including a BMW deployment and laundry) and a handful of spec numbers (height 5'8", 20 kg payload, 61 kg, 5 h runtime, 1.2 m/s). The announcement adds dense engineering numbers: 60% wider field of view, 3 g tactile threshold, 2 kW wireless charging, 9% mass reduction, a factory (BotQ) planned for 12,000 units a year. Team credibility is used as social proof ("over 100 years of combined" experience) rather than customer testimonials. CTAs are "Explore", "Watch", "Careers", email signup, app-store buttons.
- **Visual system.** Dark, cinematic, large photographic hero of the robot in homes, generous whitespace, minimal copy per screen, uppercase spec labels, video-first motion. Index-app uses a montage of everyday scenes and a globe to signal scale. Density is very low on marketing pages and high in the news post.
- **What makes it world-class / weak.** Strong: a single clear narrative (body, brain, data, factory) that every page reinforces; engineering depth lives in long-form news posts so marketing pages stay clean; spec blocks are consistent. Weak: safety is a one-line commitment on the company and Master Plan pages, with real detail only buried in the announcement (soft textiles, multi-density foam against pinch points, four layers of battery protection, cordless charging). Index-app says nothing about privacy or data rights despite collecting home video. Values copy reads like an internal poster.
- **Platform notes.** Not a platform: no SDK, API, registry or docs. Index is the closest thing to an ecosystem and it is data-in, not developer-out.
- **What Cookwala should borrow, adapt or avoid.** Borrow: the discipline of one storyline repeated on every page, and keeping deep technical prose in dated posts rather than the home page. Adapt: a spec block with five or six fixed fields for every certified kitchen or robot profile. Avoid: treating safety as a footnote; Cookwala's whole proposition is that safety is the headline, so the pinch-point, battery and charging details Figure buries should be the kind of thing Cookwala puts up front. Avoid, too, collecting home data without stating who owns it.

## 2. 1X — 1x.tech

Pages read: home, /neo, /about, /ai. (`/discover/neo-safety` does not exist.)

- **What problem it solves.** Household chores for ordinary homes, framed as giving people time back; longer-term, the site claims a path to an abundant society through general-purpose robots. The AI page frames a second problem: robots cannot be scripted for every home, so they need a model that predicts outcomes before acting.
- **How it approaches it.** One consumer product, NEO, designed around being safe to live with: a soft lattice-polymer body, tendon-driven low-inertia actuators, pinch-proof joints, low noise, splash resistance. Autonomy is supplemented by "Expert Mode", scheduled remote human supervision for tasks NEO does not yet know. The AI page describes a physics-grounded video world model as the cognitive core.
- **What it offers and to whom.** A refundable $200 deposit and an order flow for consumers; a waitlist; a chronological AI research feed for the technical audience; 92 open roles for recruits. The about page tells a 2014 to 2027 story from Halodi's industrial EVE robot to NEO in homes.
- **How it communicates.** Headlines are one or two words plus a short sub-line; copy is sparse, concrete and benefit-led, with sentences well under fifteen words. Structure on the NEO page is a logical ladder: transform the home, utility, design and safety, companionship, intelligence, then an FAQ. Proof is a spec sheet rather than logos: 66 lb, 5'6", 4 h runtime, 842 Wh, 22 dB, IP44, lifts 154 lb, carries 55 lb, 2% torque accuracy. Comparison to a familiar object (quieter than a fridge) does the persuading. CTAs: "Order Now", "Watch", "Join waitlist", "Get notified".
- **Visual system.** Warm, soft, domestic: knitted-looking robot in several colourways, lifestyle photography with older adults and families, light backgrounds, a sans-serif with wide tracking, exploded technical diagrams of the tendon drive and mesh body to balance the softness, video for design walkthroughs. Density low; one idea per screen.
- **What makes it world-class / weak.** Strong: safety is a design argument, not a disclaimer, and it is expressed in physical, checkable numbers (weight, noise, torque accuracy, ingress rating). The soft colour palette and fabric body make the safety story visible before it is read. A date-stamped AI feed signals ongoing work without clutter. Weak: no public price, no shipping date, and no dedicated safety or privacy page even though Expert Mode means a remote human may see inside your home; the about page has no team, funding or location detail beyond Palo Alto.
- **Platform notes.** No developer surface at all; closed hardware and software.
- **What Cookwala should borrow, adapt or avoid.** Borrow: the ladder structure (what it does, how it is safe, how it learns, FAQ) and the habit of translating every safety property into a number or a household comparison. Adapt: a Cookwala "safety sheet" per conformant device with the same six or seven fields (mass, max force, noise, ingress rating, hot-surface handling, cut-off latency, human override). Avoid: the absence of a standalone safety and privacy page; Cookwala should have both, and should spell out what remote-operator modes like Expert Mode are allowed to do in a kitchen.

## 3. Unitree — unitree.com

Pages read: home, /g1, /opensource, GitHub unitreerobotics/unitree_sdk2. The document centre (support.unitree.com) renders only a title to a text fetcher **[not fetched; search summary used for its contents]**.

- **What problem it solves.** Affordable, physically capable robots (quadrupeds, humanoids, arms, LiDAR) for research, education, industry inspection and entertainment, at prices far below Western competitors; the G1 humanoid is listed from $13.5K.
- **How it approaches it.** Vertical integration of motors, joints, sensors and whole robots, a wide catalogue (As2, Go2, A2, B2 quadrupeds; H2, R1, G1 humanoids; D-series arms; L2 LiDAR), and an EDU tier with secondary-development access, SDK and OTA updates. The site claims a unified robot large model (UnifoLM) and imitation and reinforcement learning drive the G1.
- **What it offers and to whom.** Catalogue plus store and dealer links for buyers; spec tables and app downloads for owners; GitHub resource centre, document centre and C++ SDK (BSD-3-Clause, Ubuntu 20.04, CMake) for developers; a DDS-based API with ROS 2 compatibility according to third-party guides. Audiences: labs, universities, integrators, hobbyists, and event producers.
- **How it communicates.** Headline style is grand and slightly poetic (product taglines about destiny and stellar exploration) layered over dense spec tables and exclamation marks. Sentences swing between very short and very long. Navigation is a deep product tree (Robots, Humanoid, Industry, Open Source, Services, Download, Challenges). Proof is spectacle: 109 robots at the Beijing 2022 Olympics opening, Spring Festival Gala performances, Asian Games, plus specs (G1: 1,320 mm, about 35 kg, 23 to 43 DoF, 9,000 mAh, about 2 h, depth camera plus 3D LiDAR). Safety appears as a footer warning that the robot is powerful and people should keep "sufficient safe distance", plus a disclaimer that the industry is early and users should understand limitations. CTAs: price, "Learn More", store and cooperate, app downloads.
- **Visual system.** High-contrast action photography and performance footage, product renders on neutral backgrounds, long single-page product sheets ending in a parameter table, dense information, little whitespace, inconsistent typography between regions and languages.
- **What makes it world-class / weak.** Strong: price transparency, complete spec tables, a real SDK repo with 1.4k stars, ROS 2 interoperability, and proof via public events anyone can look up. Weak: the open-source page is a navigation hub rather than a list of repos or datasets; the docs centre is a JS app with mixed Chinese and English; safety is a liability disclaimer rather than a design story; the SDK README does not say which robots it supports and points to a Chinese docs URL; marketing copy is uneven in English.
- **Platform notes.** Architecture: onboard locomotion computer exposing topics over CycloneDDS; unitree_sdk2 in C++ with a Python wrapper (per third-party guides) and unitree_ros2 for ROS 2 Humble/Jazzy. No formal versioning story beyond git tags; no registry; governance is single-vendor. Onboarding depends on buying the EDU tier and following a Chinese-first doc centre.
- **What Cookwala should borrow, adapt or avoid.** Borrow: always show the price or the cost of conformance, always end a product or profile page with a complete parameter table, and use DDS/ROS 2 style topic naming as a reference when describing bridges. Adapt: a one-screen "open source" page that actually lists repos, schemas and datasets with licences. Avoid: safety as a footer warning; mixed-language docs; an SDK whose README does not name the devices it supports. Cookwala's conformance suite should name every supported robot and version explicitly.

## 4. Pollen Robotics — pollen-robotics.com

Pages read: home, /reachy-mini, /about-us/, docs.pollen-robotics.com (Reachy 2 docs), GitHub pollen-robotics/reachy_mini.

- **What problem it solves.** Making an expressive, hackable robot cheap and open enough that AI builders, makers, educators and researchers can run their own models on real hardware and share the results.
- **How it approaches it.** Two small, affordable products (Reachy Mini at $499 wireless or $399 Lite, shipped as a two-hour DIY kit; Microduck, a $399 biped for RL), plus a larger research platform (Reachy 2) with VR teleoperation. Everything is open: Apache 2.0 code, CC BY-SA-NC hardware, Python and JavaScript SDKs, a MuJoCo simulator, and distribution through Hugging Face Spaces for apps and datasets. The site claims 10,000+ Reachy Minis shipped, 60+ community apps and 400+ shared "moves".
- **What it offers and to whom.** Hardware kits and a store; an app store with one-click install; a community dataset model where a recorded motion becomes a dataset any robot can replay; Discord, GitHub and Hugging Face docs for builders; discounts for open-source projects and free help for environmental projects per the about page. Audience is explicitly makers and developers (the Lite variant is labelled for developers only).
- **How it communicates.** Headlines are short, warm and imperative; sub-lines explain the use case in one sentence. Structure on the product page: hero with dual CTA (buy or get started), two entry paths (build or install apps), pricing, conversation, apps, community moves, community, final CTA. Proof is community numbers plus logos (Hugging Face), the ANA Avatar XPRIZE second place, seed funding of 2.4 million euros, and a 2016 to 2024 timeline. CTAs: buy, get started, join Discord, browse apps, build your own app.
- **Visual system.** Playful illustrated robots in costumes rather than product photography, bright accent colours on white, rounded friendly type, animated character moments, low density. The docs use a conventional five-tab developer layout (getting started, hardware, developing, teleoperation, help) with search and a prominent "Try in Simulation" path.
- **What makes it world-class / weak.** Strong: the lowest barrier to first success of any site here (a few lines of code to move the head; simulator needs no hardware; apps install in one click); an honest, humble company voice; a docs site that starts with safety guidelines in Getting Started and keeps older product generations online. Weak: the main product page has no safety mention at all (mitigated by the small size of the robots); the about page is dated and does not describe the Hugging Face relationship clearly; versioning is implied by git history rather than stated releases.
- **Platform notes.** Architecture: SDK over USB or Wi-Fi to a Raspberry Pi CM4, or to a MuJoCo sim; apps are web/JS deployed via Hugging Face Spaces; datasets in community format. Licences stated clearly per layer. Registry is the Hugging Face hub rather than a Pollen-run index. Governance is company-led with community contribution; there is an AGENTS.md for AI-assisted development. Onboarding: install uv, follow a five-minute SDK guide, run the hello-world, then progress to AI tutorials.
- **What Cookwala should borrow, adapt or avoid.** Borrow: dual hero CTAs (read the spec vs run the quickstart), a "try in simulation" path that needs no hardware (Cookwala already has simulators; surface them first), the community dataset idea (a recipe layer or safety envelope published once and replayable by any conformant device), and layer-by-layer licence clarity. Adapt: an app-store style registry of Cookwala recipe layers and bridges hosted on an existing hub rather than a bespoke one. Avoid: product pages with no safety line; even friendly robots in kitchens need one.

## 5. NVIDIA Isaac — developer.nvidia.com/isaac, Isaac Sim, Isaac Lab, GR00T

Pages read: /isaac, /isaac/sim, /isaac/lab, /isaac/gr00t, docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com, isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab, GitHub NVIDIA/Isaac-GR00T.

- **What problem it solves.** Building robot AI is slow because real-world data is scarce and testing on hardware is risky; Isaac offers simulation, synthetic data, learning frameworks, foundation models and accelerated runtimes so developers can train in simulation and deploy on NVIDIA hardware.
- **How it approaches it.** A layered stack: Isaac Sim (open-source reference simulator on Omniverse/USD, Apache 2.0), Isaac Lab (GPU-accelerated robot-learning framework, BSD-3-Clause, with environments for many robot morphologies), Isaac ROS and CUDA libraries, GR00T (open VLA foundation model for humanoids, code Apache 2.0, weights under the NVIDIA Open Model License), Cosmos world models for augmentation, and Jetson Thor for on-robot inference. Everything is positioned as a reference you extend.
- **What it offers and to whom.** Free downloads via GitHub, pip and containers; cloud instances; a learning library, self-paced courses, office-hours livestreams, forums and Discord; starter kits per use case (synthetic data, ROS, digital twins, policy learning). Audiences: roboticists, ML engineers, ISVs and enterprises. Partner logos include Boston Dynamics, 1X, Agility, AgiBot, Fourier, Google DeepMind, Disney Research and the Linux Foundation (Newton physics).
- **How it communicates.** Headlines are plain descriptions of what each tool is, usually one long sentence packed with qualifiers; a question-style hook on the hub page. Sentences are 8 to 15 words in body copy, longer in headlines. Structure is identical on every product page: how it works, get started, extend, starter kits, learning library, news, resources, FAQ. Proof is partner logos, open licences, a peer-reviewed paper with BibTeX, and explicit version numbers (Isaac Lab 2.3 preview on the marketing page, 3.0 in docs; GR00T N1.5, N1.6, N1.7 on named branches). CTAs: "Download Now from GitHub", read documentation, watch a 2-minute video, take the course.
- **Visual system.** Corporate NVIDIA green on black and white, rendered simulation scenes and digital-twin imagery, card grids, dense navigation, standard Sphinx-style docs with version selector. Motion limited to video embeds. Density high everywhere.
- **What makes it world-class / weak.** Strong: the most complete platform discipline of the five: version selectors, release notes, known issues, migration guides across versions, four installation routes with a recommended default, three canonical first tutorials, hardware requirements stated as GPU memory, citation and acknowledgement of the project it forked from (Orbit), and a data schema (LeRobot v2 plus a modality file) documented in the repo. Weak: marketing headlines are jargon-heavy and trademark-laden; the hub page lacks a single obvious first action; licensing is split across Apache, BSD and a proprietary model licence, which the site does not reconcile in one place; the whole stack assumes NVIDIA GPUs.
- **Platform notes.** Architecture: USD-native sim, pluggable physics engines (PhysX, Newton), RL and IL workflows, server-client inference for GR00T. API: documented Python modules per subsystem. SDKs: pip packages, containers, source. Registry: an environments catalogue in Isaac Lab and models on Hugging Face. Versioning: semantic releases, branches per model generation, migration guides. Governance: NVIDIA-led open source with contribution guidelines; Newton is under the Linux Foundation. Onboarding: a recommended quick install, three tutorials, courses, office hours.
- **What Cookwala should borrow, adapt or avoid.** Borrow: the full docs discipline (version selector, release notes, known issues, migration guide per breaking change, one recommended install path and three canonical first tutorials), a published data schema with a small demo dataset in the repo, a citation block, and starter kits organised by job to be done (home cook, kitchen operator, robot vendor, regulator). Adapt: a Cookwala conformance "office hours" or recorded walkthrough. Avoid: trademark-stuffed one-sentence headlines, and licence sprawl; Cookwala should state one licence for the spec and one for reference code on a single page.

## Patterns across these five

- **Consumer sites sell a feeling and hide the spec; platform sites sell the spec and hide the feeling.** Figure and 1X lead with lifestyle imagery and five-field spec blocks; NVIDIA and Pollen lead with what you can run today. Cookwala needs both registers on separate pages: a calm public page about safe kitchens and a dense developer page about envelopes, schemas and conformance.
- **Safety is almost always under-communicated.** Only 1X makes safety the design argument, and even it has no safety page. Figure buries it in a news post; Unitree uses a footer disclaimer; Pollen's product page is silent; NVIDIA treats it as sim-to-real. That gap is Cookwala's opening: make the safety sheet the hero.
- **Numbers beat adjectives.** The most credible lines are measurable (22 dB, 3 g tactile threshold, 16 GB VRAM, $13.5K, 10,000 shipped). Cookwala's limits and envelopes should be shown as numbers with units, never as "safe".
- **Every good platform has one recommended first path.** Quick Install, "Try in Simulation", three lines of Python. Cookwala should pick one: run the home simulator with the protocol on, in under five minutes, with no hardware.
- **Open licences are stated per layer and up front.** Apache 2.0 for code, BSD-3 for SDKs, CC for hardware, a separate licence for weights. Cookwala should put the royalty-free spec licence and the reference-code licence in the footer of every page and at the top of the repo.
- **Proof comes from outside the company.** Olympic ceremonies, XPRIZE placements, partner logos, peer-reviewed papers, community counts. Cookwala should seek a small number of external anchors (a university lab, a standards body, a public conformance run) rather than claims about itself.
- **Versioning is a trust signal.** NVIDIA's version selector, release notes and migration guides do more to signal seriousness than any headline. Cookwala Core should expose version, changelog and a migration note for every schema change.
- **Page structure repeats across a family of products.** NVIDIA's identical section order for Sim, Lab and GR00T, and 1X's ladder, make sites learnable. Cookwala should fix one template for every profile (people, kitchens, robots) and every bridge.
