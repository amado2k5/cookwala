# Dignity in automated care

The strongest case for a cooking machine is not convenience. It is the person who wants to cook
for themselves and cannot use a stove today: someone recovering from a stroke who wants their own
food, a woman of ninety who has fed others all her life and would rather feed herself, a young
adult whose kitchen was not built for them. For them a machine that cooks safely is not a luxury.
It is the difference between being fed and eating.

That is also where the risk of indignity is highest. A machine can feed a person without
asking them anything. It can decide what is good for them from a profile. It can serve a
tray and leave. It can be the reason nobody else comes.

Cookwala has made a few choices here and left most of the hard questions open.

**The person chooses; the machine checks.** Nothing in the standard computes what a person
should eat. Rule packs flag sodium or sugar on a menu and say where the rule came from; they
do not decide the menu. Clinician-set targets are entered locally by a clinician; the machine
never diagnoses. Weight goals are opt-in; the default is "eat well". A person who wants the
salty dish they have eaten for seventy years is told the number and served the dish.

**Texture and serving are part of the recipe, not an afterthought.** A recipe can carry a
texture level, a serving form and a serving temperature, so a meal for someone who swallows
with difficulty is the same dish prepared properly, not a different, lesser meal.

**Presence is a safety rule, not a social policy.** Steps that cannot be verified by a sensor
need a person present. We have resisted the temptation to make presence a requirement for
other reasons, because a rule that says "a person must be there" for someone who lives alone
is a rule that denies them the machine. The social question of who visits is not solved by a
schema and should not be hidden inside one.

**The hard case, stated plainly.** The sensor ladder's last rung for sautéing, searing, frying
and reducing is a person. The people this essay is written for are often the people who cannot
be that person: they cannot stand at the pan, see the colour or smell the smoke. For them the
current standard refuses exactly the steps that make a meal taste like home, and "will you
watch?" is a question they cannot answer with yes. We do not have a rule that closes this gap.
The honest options are better sensors on cheaper devices, a remote attendant who is reachable
and accountable, or a recipe written to avoid those steps; each has a cost in taste, in money or
in dignity. It is listed as an open problem, not solved by this essay.

**No score of a person, ever.** The household context profile forbids behavioural scoring.
How fast someone eats, what they leave on the plate, whether they argue at dinner: a machine
may notice these to serve them better and may never turn them into a number about them, and
may never send them anywhere.

**The cook's name stays on the plate.** A machine that cooks a dish from a care home's own
cook, or the resident's own recipe, serves something that belongs to someone. We think this
matters more than it sounds.

What we have not answered: whether being fed by a machine, however well, is a loss that
should be named rather than designed around; whether the people who do this work today should
be protected from the machines or given them as tools; who speaks for the person who cannot
answer the machine's question. The standard's governance reserves a seat for a labour or
consumer voice; a seat for the people served, including disabled users, is proposed and not yet
in the charter. That is a start, not an answer.
