Cookwala / Why

Why an open standard for cooking, and why now

Three things are true at once. Machines are starting to cook. Food is thrown away while people go hungry. And the most intimate dataset in any home is about to be collected by appliances. Cookwala is one small, open answer to all three.

The problem, in four parts

1. Machines are learning to move; nobody wrote down how to cook

Home and kitchen robots are shipping or taking orders. Each maker writes its own closed recipes, mostly from a few cuisines, and decides alone what "simmer" means and when chicken is safe. There is no shared, checkable definition of "done" or "never".

2. Unsafe food and unsafe kitchens

Unsafe food causes about 600 million illnesses and 420,000 deaths a year measured · WHO. Cooking machines add new ways to be wrong: hot oil estimated by a clock, a recipe that says 240 °C, an agent that obeys text it read.

3. Food is thrown away while people go hungry

About 13 % of food is lost between harvest and retail measured · FAO; about 1.05 billion tonnes were wasted in 2022 measured · UNEP; about 733 million people faced hunger in 2023 measured · SOFI. Food banks rescue what they can with phone calls and spreadsheets that differ by site.

4. The most intimate dataset ever collected

A robot that cooks well knows a household's schedules, layout, children, health, religion and budget. No rule says what it may do with them, or who else may know.

The answer: three things a machine can check

What to make. A recipe as typed steps with a shared vocabulary of operations, each with a physical envelope: simmer is water at 85 to 96 °C; deep frying is oil at 160 to 190 °C.

When it's done. Every heat step ends on something a device can measure: a temperature, a food-state cue, a time window, with a defined path when it times out.

What must never happen. Safety limits enforced on the device itself. No recipe, agent, message or update can raise them. A device that cannot verify a step refuses before it heats anything. No oil thermometer means no deep frying.

Around that core: signed records anyone can check; agents that act only under a mandate and treat all text as data; a profile for food banks that carries no personal data; a profile that keeps a household's facts at home.

Why open, why now

Standards that arrive after the products are written by the products' makers. The window to write the rules for cooking machines in the open, before they are in millions of homes, is the next few years. An open standard lets a small maker, a food bank in Cairo and a university lab use the same format as the largest company, and lets regulators point at one thing.

Cookwala is royalty-free, model-neutral and device-neutral, governed in public today and headed for a neutral foundation. It started from one family's Egyptian recipes on fifi.cooking. todo The founder's origin story, in their own words, goes here.

Three goals, told honestly

Help end hunger

Hunger is driven by poverty, conflict, climate shocks, prices and policy. Cookwala's part is real and partial: surplus reaching a plate safely, programs that can compare their numbers, and later, farms and stores that plan to need. We never claim Cookwala ends hunger.

Make people healthier

Food-safety limits a device must meet and record; nutrition rules a program can apply and a dietitian can review. General guidance only; nothing medical.

Put robots to work for people

Robots that cook every cuisine safely, learn with consent and credit, serve people who cannot cook for themselves, and give human time back, with a labour voice in governance.

Three horizons, without hype

Now, to about 2028

  • Safer devices that refuse rather than guess
  • Recipes that work across machines, with the cook's name on them
  • Food banks rescuing more, safely, with phones and spreadsheets
  • Agents that can't be tricked by text in a recipe
  • Open tools you can run today

Next, to about 2036

  • Home and commercial robots that cook every cuisine
  • A consented dataset that keeps cultural recipes alive
  • New roles: recipe engineers, food-robot technicians, certifiers, rule-pack reviewers
  • Less waste; cheaper, healthier meals
  • A standard regulators point to

Later

  • Cooking as infrastructure: nutritious food available to anyone, anywhere, including disaster zones and people who can't cook for themselves
  • Less waste, lower emissions
  • Human time returned to people
  • Machines that inherit the world's culinary heritage instead of erasing it

Each horizon is a direction, not a date or a count. What exists today is listed on the roadmap with a status per item.

What we got wrong so far

The first one-liner ("the world's first and largest robot cooking recipes index") overstated what existed. The first schemas accepted unknown fields. The first simulators used a strawman baseline.
